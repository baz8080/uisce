What this measures

This site tracks what Uisce Éireann announces, not what happens at your tap. Every duration is counted from when a notice was published — for an unplanned burst the water was already off before that. The headline “works complete” time is a minimum: it counts only the notices that reported one. The availability percentages are a best estimate, because a total has to put a number on every disruption, including the ones that never reported an end.

How these numbers are worked out

How long a disruption lasted

The time from notice publication to a stated “works are now complete” update, over disruption incidents that carry one. Incidents whose only end signal was a scheduled finish are counted separately and shown as “scheduled”: a published plan is not evidence the works ended then, so the two are never pooled into a single headline. End times are read from the notice text by an LLM (see the accuracy evaluation); no time on this page is a measurement taken in the field.

About one incident in twenty never reports an end at all — the count is shown beside the headline each month. Those are left out of the figure above, because including them would mean counting an estimate as a confirmation. They are not the long ones being quietly hidden: checked both ways, they run shorter than average, so leaving them out moves the headline up, not down. They are still counted in full in the availability percentages and person-hours, where leaving something out means calling it zero rather than calling it unknown.

What counts as a disruption

Only hard disruptions count against the availability figures: bursts, reservoir / treatment-plant / pump-station interruptions, pump failures, power outages, and unplanned repair works. Investigations, leak detection, hydrant works and installations are shown as works but never count; restrictions, low pressure and discolouration are shown but never count. A closed notice that never reported a usable end is charged the typical length of a completed notice of the same kind of works — a burst main and a hydrant repair get different figures — rather than nothing at all.

Repeating works

Where a notice describes works repeating over a range of nights or days — “daily from 10pm until 7am, from 9 to 27 July” — only the hours inside those windows are counted, so the incident reads as the hours the works took rather than the days they spanned. A notice announcing a window that repeats like this is treated as a restriction whatever its title says, because the same supply zone is published sometimes as a conservation restriction and sometimes as an interruption, and the two describe the same thing.

How many people a notice affects

Each notice pin is assumed to affect the Census 2022 Small Areas whose centres lie within 500 m (CSO SAPS populations); the headline percentage is the population-weighted share of people's time not covered by an announced supply disruption. County rankings are robust to the 500 m assumption; the letter grades are calibrated to it. Each notice is also capped at 14 days, so an incident published as several staggered notices can span longer than that.

What the letters mean

Supply availability over the month, and nothing else:

  • A 99.9% or better
  • B 99.75% or better
  • C 99.45% or better
  • D 99.0% or better
  • F below 99.0%

Why the ! sits beside the grade, not inside it

An active boil-water / do-not-drink / do-not-consume notice is shown as a marker beside the grade rather than folded into it: whether the water is safe to drink is a different question from how much of it there was, and a letter cannot answer both.

The ten largest

The ten largest disruptions of each finished month rank the same hard disruptions as individual incidents, with person-hours clipped to the month so they sum against the county figures. A notice reported “confirmed” there is one carrying a completion update, and since an incident is often published as several notices with different end signals, the count of confirmed notices is shown rather than a single verdict.

Changes to how this site measures

15 August 2026. About one disruption in twenty never reports when it ended. Those used to be recorded as one second long, which counted a real outage as having disrupted nobody. They are now charged the typical length of a completed notice for the same kind of works, and how many were estimated is shown beside the headline each month. National availability falls by one to two hundredths of a percentage point; the worst-hit county-month falls by two tenths, and four county-months move down one grade. The headline “works complete” time is unchanged in four months of five and rose 0.2h in the fifth — these incidents stay out of it, but five large disruptions were published as several notices of which only some reported an end, and those now carry an estimate for the rest.

2 August 2026. An active boil-water or do-not-drink notice used to knock the grade down one step. It no longer does — that made a county with a notice affecting 200 people indistinguishable from one that lost supply county-wide. The notice is now shown as a marker beside the grade instead.
