What this measures

This site tracks what Uisce Éireann announces, not what happens at your tap. A notice counts once, however many pins it is published as, in the county it names. The count ignores size: a burst on a village main and one on a city trunk main each count one.

The typical time to “works complete” is the only measure of how long, and it is a minimum: it runs from when a notice was published, not when the service was lost, and counts only the notices that reported completion.

Water main lengths are Uisce Éireann’s own, from its Water Supply Zones, added up by the council each zone belongs to. A few zones reach over a county line - Drogheda’s, run from Louth, serves part of Meath - so a county’s length is its council’s mains, not exactly the mains inside its border.

Simply put: an outage notice is a burst, a supply interruption or an unplanned repair. Restrictions, low pressure, quality notices and planned works are shown but not counted.

How these numbers are worked out

How long a disruption lasted

The time from a notice going up to its stated “works are now complete” update. Incidents whose only end signal was a scheduled finish are counted separately - a published plan is not evidence the works ended then. End times are read from the notice text (see the accuracy evaluation); no time on this page is a measurement taken in the field.

About one incident in twenty never reports an end at all - the count is shown beside the county’s outage count each month. Those stay out of the figure above: checked both ways, they run shorter than average, so leaving them out moves the typical duration up, not down.

When a notice closed

A notice counts as closed on the day its own update reports the works complete. One that never says so counts as closed the first time a twice-daily check finds it gone, so those dates run late and the closed-in-month lists are minimums.

What counts as an outage

Bursts, reservoir / treatment-plant / pump-station interruptions, pump failures, power outages and unplanned repairs. Restrictions, low pressure, discolouration and routine works are shown but never counted. A notice belongs to the month it was first published in.

Repeating works

Works announced as a repeating window - “daily from 10pm until 7am, from 9 to 27 July” - are treated as a restriction whatever the title says, so they are shown but not counted.

What the letters mean

Outage notices published per 100 km of water main in the month:

  • A fewer than 1
  • B fewer than 2
  • C fewer than 3
  • D fewer than 4
  • E fewer than 5
  • F 5 or more

The month under way is graded on the last 30 days, so a letter does not open every month at A; it can change between updates with nothing new published, as older notices leave the window. The cut-offs are whole numbers, chosen to be read, and reviewed once a year. April 2026, which this site saw only from the 20th, has no letter.

Why the ! sits beside the grade, not inside it

Whether the water is safe to drink is a different question from how often supply is cut, and a letter cannot answer both - so an active boil-water / do-not-drink / do-not-consume notice is a marker beside the grade, never folded into it.

Changes to how this site measures

6 October 2026. Counties used to be graded on an estimate: each notice was charged to everyone living within a fixed distance of its pin, for as long as it stood, and the letter was the share of people’s time no notice covered. The feed never says how many people a notice reaches, and the fixed distance charged a city notice about ten times the people of a rural one. The letters now count outage notices per 100 km of water main, which the feed and Uisce Éireann’s own records do say. The population-weighted figures are gone, and the ten largest disruptions, which were ranked on them, are withdrawn until they can be ranked on something the feed says.

15 August 2026. Disruptions that never report an end used to count as one second long, which recorded a real outage as having disrupted nobody. They were then charged the typical length of completed works of the same kind. The national figure moved by hundredths of a point; four county-months dropped one grade.

2 August 2026. An active boil-water or do-not-drink notice used to knock the grade down one step. It no longer does - a notice affecting 200 people read the same as a county that lost supply county-wide. It is now a marker beside the grade instead.
