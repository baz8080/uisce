What this measures

This site tracks what Uisce Éireann announces, not what happens at your tap. Durations count from when a notice was published, not when the service was lost. The published “works complete” time is a minimum: it counts only the notices that reported one. The availability percentages are a best estimate, because a total has to put a number on every disruption, including the ones that never reported an end.

How these numbers are worked out

How long a disruption lasted

The time from a notice going up to its stated “works are now complete” update. Incidents whose only end signal was a scheduled finish are counted separately - a published plan is not evidence the works ended then. End times are read from the notice text (see the accuracy evaluation); no time on this page is a measurement taken in the field.

About one incident in twenty never reports an end at all - the count is shown beside the county's disruption count each month. Those stay out of the figure above: checked both ways, they run shorter than average, so leaving them out moves the typical duration up, not down. They still count in full in the availability percentages and person-hours, where leaving something out would mean calling it zero.

When a notice closed

A notice counts as closed the first time a twice-daily check finds it gone, so close dates are approximate and the closed-in-month lists are minimums.

What counts as a disruption

Only hard disruptions count against availability: bursts, reservoir / treatment-plant / pump-station interruptions, pump failures, power outages and unplanned repairs. Restrictions, low pressure, discolouration and routine works are shown but never count. A closed notice with no usable end is charged the typical length of a completed notice of the same kind of works, rather than nothing at all.

Repeating works

Works announced as a repeating window - “daily from 10pm until 7am, from 9 to 27 July” - are charged only the hours inside the windows, and are treated as a restriction whatever the title says.

How many people a notice affects

Each notice is assumed to affect the Census 2022 Small Areas whose centres lie within 500 m of its pin. Availability weighs each notice by how many people it covers and how long it stands: the percentage is the share of all that person-time no notice covered. One notice is charged at most 14 days.

What the letters mean

Supply availability over the month, and nothing else:

  • A 99.9% or better
  • B 99.75% or better
  • C 99.45% or better
  • D 99.0% or better
  • E 98.7% or better
  • F below 98.7%

Why the ! sits beside the grade, not inside it

Whether the water is safe to drink is a different question from how much of it there was, and a letter cannot answer both - so an active boil-water / do-not-drink / do-not-consume notice is a marker beside the grade, never folded into it.

The ten largest

The ten largest disruptions of each finished month rank the same hard disruptions as individual incidents, with person-hours clipped to the month so they sum against the county figures.

Changes to how this site measures

15 August 2026. Disruptions that never report an end used to count as one second long, which recorded a real outage as having disrupted nobody. They are now charged the typical length of completed works of the same kind, with the estimated count shown each month. National availability moves by hundredths of a point; four county-months drop one grade.

2 August 2026. An active boil-water or do-not-drink notice used to knock the grade down one step. It no longer does - a notice affecting 200 people read the same as a county that lost supply county-wide. It is now a marker beside the grade instead.
